About us

We cook with fire, butter, and patience.

Ember Butter Plates is an independent recipe blog for home cooks who love depth, smoke, and honest food. We write about techniques we use, ingredients we buy, and the stories behind the meals that matter.

Our story

Ember Butter Plates started in 2021 around a beat‑up cast iron skillet and a shared frustration: too many recipe blogs were long on stories and short on technique. We wanted a place where the recipe actually worked, where the method was explained, and where the writing didn’t get in the way of the food.

So a group of former line cooks, pastry chefs, and cast‑iron collectors started testing. Every recipe on this site is cooked at least three times in a home kitchen — not a professional test kitchen with someone else doing the dishes. We burn things. We fix them. We write it all down.

The name? It comes from the moment a knob of butter hits a screaming‑hot pan and starts to foam and brown. That’s the ember. That’s where flavour lives.

What we believe

  • Test, test, test. A recipe isn’t ready until it’s been made at least three times, by different cooks, on different stoves.
  • Technique over gadgets. A heavy skillet and a sharp knife will take you further than any single‑use appliance.
  • Butter is an ingredient, not a garnish. We use it purposefully — browned, cultured, compounded, or foaming.
  • Honest writing. No 2,000‑word life story before the ingredients. Just clear instructions, context, and the why.
  • Cast iron is a relationship. It rewards patience and punishes neglect. We’re here for both.
  • Everyone can cook. Curiosity beats culinary school. A willingness to learn beats expensive equipment.

How we test recipes

We don’t have a test kitchen with a dozen stoves. We have home kitchens, a notebook, and a habit of cooking the same dish until it’s right. Here’s what that looks like:

  • Round 1: One cook makes the recipe exactly as written, taking notes on timing, temperature, and anything that feels off.
  • Round 2: A second cook makes it in a different kitchen with different equipment, to catch assumptions about pan size or stove heat.
  • Round 3: A third cook makes it for friends or family, and we watch what they do. If they deviate, we ask why — and adjust.

Only then do we photograph it, write it up, and publish. If a recipe fails repeatedly, we either fix it or shelve it. We’d rather publish nothing than publish something that doesn’t work.
